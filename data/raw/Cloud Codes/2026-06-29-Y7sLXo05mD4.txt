So what really happens when you run that line? Every job FFMPEG does, every single one of them follows the same five step pipeline. Dmux, decode, filter, encode, and mux. Let us walk through it carefully, one stage at a time. Step 1, Dmux. A video file is really just a box, a container holding several separate streams sitting side by side. The picture lives in one track, the sound in another, and maybe subtitles in a third. Dmuxing simply opens that box and pulls those streams apart. Step 2, decode. That video stream is heavily compressed. It is not real pictures yet, just a clever set of instructions for rebuilding them. The decoder follows those instructions and turns the data back into actual frames. The raw, uncompressed pixels, the FFMPEG can finally work with directly. Step 3 is the fun one, filters. This is where you reshape the picture itself. Scale it, crop it, rotate it, drop a watermark on top, change the frame rate. You chain these effects together into what is called a filter graph, and every single frame flows straight through it in order. Step 4, encode. Raw frames are enormous, far too big to store or to send, so FFMPEG squeezes them back down using a codec, like H264, H265, or the newer AV1. This is the magic step that takes a gigabyte and turns it into a few megabytes, without your eye ever catching the difference. Step 5, mux. The exact opposite of Dmux, the freshly encoded video and audio get packed back together into one new container, perfectly lined up in time, open the box, rebuild the contents, seal a fresh box. That in five short words is FFMPEG. And here's the thing that almost everyone mixes up. A container, like MP4 or MKV, is just the box. The codec, like H264 or AV1, is the language being spoken inside it. The same box can hold completely different languages, and understanding that one distinction is honestly half the battle.
