Here's a question that just got a $4 billion answer. Is Mojo actually better than Kuda? On one side, in Vidya's Kuda, the software that has quietly run almost all of artificial intelligence for nearly two decades. On the other, Mojo, a brand new language that claims it can finally replace it, and on June 24th, 2026, Qualcomm stopped watching and made its move. It's acquiring modular, the company behind Mojo for around $3.9 billion entirely in stock. That is an enormous price for a programming language almost nobody outside AI infrastructure had even heard of. But understand this, it was never really about a language. This is an attack aimed at the single most valuable mode in all of technology. Because the company sitting in the crosshairs is right now worth $4.6 trillion. And here's what most people get wrong about in Vidya. Its real moat was never the chips themselves. It's Kuda, the software layer on top, and the 7.5 million developers who are now locked inside it. Because Kuda has one unbreakable rule, it runs only on in Vidya hardware. Want to use an AMD card, an Intel accelerator, a Qualcomm chip? You simply can't. Your code is welded to one company's silicon forever. And that rule creates a painful split. You prototype your model in Python because Python is easy. Then you rewrite all the fast parts in C++ and Kuda because Python is slow. Two languages, two teams, and a giant wall sitting in the middle of every AI project. Mojo's entire pitch is to delete that wall. One language, one file. Python simplicity sitting on top, bare metal performance underneath, the same code all the way from your research notebook down to the raw GPU. And the headline number is genuinely wild. Mojo, up to 35,000 times faster than Python. But hold on, keep your skepticism. That's measured against naive, single threaded Python, and only after a long hand-tuning marathon. We'll do the honest math in a minute. So who is actually making this bet? This man, Chris Latner. You may never have heard the name, but you've used his work every single day. He built LLVM. He built clang. He built Apple Swift and Google's MLIR, the plumbing beneath modern computing. And the real weapon isn't Mojo by itself. It's max. Modular's engine that takes one single container of code and runs it, completely unchanged on Nvidia, on AMD, and on Apple chips. No Kuda anywhere. That is the actual threat to Nvidia. To understand why that's dangerous, rewind to 2007. That's the year Nvidia shipped Kuda nearly 20 years ago. While the rest of the industry saw a graphics card for gaming, Nvidia quietly turned that same chip into a supercomputer you could actually program. And then they spent 18 relentless years building on top of it. Kube Labs for linear algebra, KU-DNN for deep learning, Cutlass, Tensor ET, and thousands of hand-tuned kernels that simply work out of the box. It became a mountain no rival has managed to climb. As one industry analyst put it bluntly, Nvidia's real mode has never been the GPU's. It's Kuda and the sheer rewrite cost that keeps your workloads pinned to their hardware. In other words, the lock-in isn't a side effect. The lock-in is the product. And the numbers prove the strategy works. $4.6 trillion in market value, roughly 86% of all data center AI chips. 7.5 million developers who now think natively in Kuda. That right there is the wall everyone is trying to climb. And plenty have tried to tear it down. AMD's Rock M. Open AI's Triton. Intel's one API. Google's XLA. OpenCL. SYCL. Real engineering. Serious money. Years of effort. And not one of them has truly broken Nvidia's grip on the market. So what actually makes Mojo different from all of them? Mojo is a member of the Python family. Your existing Python code is very nearly valid Mojo, but it has the raw performance of C++ or Rust built directly into the language itself. Remember that wall between Python and C++? Engineers call it the two language problem. And it has taxed AI development for over a decade. Easy but slow on one side. Fast but brutal on the other. Mojo's core bet is simple. You should never have to choose between them. In Mojo, you start with ordinary Python. Then only where the code is hot. You add static types. You add Cindy vector instructions. You reach straight for the GPU. Line by line in the very same file. No rewrite. No porting. No second language. And the real secret is MLIR. The compiler technology, Lattner, himself created back at Google. It lets a single Mojo program target ordinary CPUs, Nvidia GPUs, AMD GPUs, Google's CPUs, and custom chips all compiled from one identical source file. Under the hood, Mojo borrows the best ideas in computing, an ownership model for memory safety, much like Rust. First class vectorization for speed and compile time meta programming, meaning the language can specialize in tune itself to whatever hardware it's about to run on. Now the honest performance truth, because this matters, yes, against naive Python, the fully tuned demo hits tens of thousands of times faster. But against the optimized numpy that engineers actually use, independent tests show handwritten Mojo matrix code running several times slower. The headline figures are best case, not typical. And Mojo is still young. As of June 2026, it sits at version 1.0 beta 2. The standard library went open source back in 2024. The compiler itself is only promised to follow later this year, deeply promising, but very much still under construction. But here's the part that should genuinely worry Nvidia. Mojo is just the language. Max is the actual product, a complete AI serving engine that compiles your model and speaks the exact same API as open AI, so it drops straight into the stack you already run today. And Max does precisely the thing CUDA for bits. The exact same container running the exact same code executes on Nvidia, on AMD, and on Apple Silicon with literally zero changes. Suddenly, the hardware becomes a choice you make, not a cage you're trapped in. Modular's own benchmarks are loud and specific. Nearly 3900 tokens per second on a single A100. Up to 53% more pre-filled throughput than VLLM on AMD's MI 300X. Their vendor numbers, sure, but the portability behind them is real and independently measured. And that one capability flips the entire economic equation. If the same software runs everywhere, you simply buy whichever chip is cheapest, fastest, or just actually in stock. The CUDA lock quietly snaps open. And that, exactly that, is what Qualcomm just paid $4 billion for. Qualcomm already had the hardware, from tiny edge and PUs all the way up to data center racks. What it badly lacked was the software layer. Modular is that missing layer. Put all the pieces together, and the strategy snaps into focus. Nvidia sells you a vertical stack, their chip, their software, their rules, top to bottom. Qualcomm and Modular are betting hard on the exact opposite, and open horizontal layer that runs on absolutely anyone's silicon. So is Mojo actually better than CUDA? Let's be honest, and let's actually score it. Raw performance on a single Nvidia GPU today, CUDA clearly wins.
