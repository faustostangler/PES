Watch this. A full English sentence is typing itself out, letter by letter, and nobody is touching the keyboard, no hands, no voice, no implant anywhere in this person's head. Every one of those letters came from a single place, raw brain activity picked up from outside the skull while the person simply tried to type. This is Brain 2 Quarty Version 2. From Metta's AI Lab, published at the very end of June 2026, and it is genuinely a big deal. Here's what makes it wild. There is no surgery, nothing is implanted, no scalpel goes anywhere near the brain. The signal is read straight from the air around your head. On the hardest test, decoding natural sentences nobody rehearsed. It hits a 39% word error rate. And for the best participant, half of all sentences come back with one wrong word, or none at all. But the real headline is this curve. The more brain data they feed it, the lower the error drops. Steadily, predictably, the accuracy is not stuck. It scales. And here's the part you are going to love. Some of the code that reads the brain was written by AI agents, cursor, running clawed, improving the decoder on its own. Which raises the question everyone is already asking, is this the no surgery answer to Neuralink? One catch up front. Today, reading these signals needs a brain scanner roughly the size of a small room. This is a lab result, not an app on your phone. So let us break it down properly. How it actually works, why it matters for real people, and whether it truly competes with the chip in your skull. First, the experiment. Nine people, 10 hours each, sitting inside a brain scanner. 22,000 sentences typed one at a time, while the machine recorded the faint magnetic flicker of their neurons firing. Here is a detail I love. Those sentences were not random. They were generated by Lama 4, Metta's own language model. So an AI wrote the sentences, a human brain typed them, and another AI decoded them back from Neural signal. The loop closes on itself, and that word magnetic is the whole trick. The scanner, called M-E-G, listens to the tiny magnetic fields your neurons give off. It is not reading your private thoughts, it is reading the motor plan, your brain commanding your fingers to hit each key, why a giant magnetic scanner, and not the simple cap of electrodes. Because signal quality is everything. The cheaper option, E-E-G, is so smeared by your skull that its error rate blows past 60%. M-E-G sees the brain far more sharply, and sharp signal is what makes real decoding possible. So how do you turn that messy magnetic signal into clean text? Brain 2, Cordy version 2, runs a three-stage pipeline. Brain signal goes in one end, a real sentence comes out the other. Let us walk each stage. Stage 1, the encoder. A convolutional network reads the raw signal from all 300 plus sensors, and a sequence model guesses the stream of keystrokes, which key, in which order. On its own, it is noisy, jittery, and full of mistakes. Stage 2, the aligner. Using contrastive learning, it links chunks of brain signal directly to whole words, teaching the model what a word looks like in your neural data, not just one letter at a time. Stage 3, a language model. The team fine tunes an LLM built on QN to take those noisy keystroke guesses, plus the neural word hints, and rewrite them into the sentence a human most likely meant. The model already knows what real English sounds like, put it together and you get this. The true sentence on top, the decoded one below. Mostly correct, and where it slips, it is usually one small word. Exactly the kind of error your phone's auto correct already fixes 100 times a day, and for the best participant, it is genuinely uncanny. Whole sentences it has never seen before, reconstructed perfectly, purely from the brain. Half the time, the worst it does is miss by a single word. Now the twist that makes this a cloud code story. To squeeze out more accuracy, the team did not just hand-tune the model themselves. They pointed three AI coding agents at it, cursor, driven by Claude Opus 4.6, and gave them one job, minimize the error. The agents rewrote the training code, launched their own experiments, and rediscovered real machine learning techniques without being told. Label smoothing, modality dropout, beam search, each one shaving the error down a little more, round after round, and they did not hide any of it. The full code is on GitHub, open for anyone, the model, the pipeline, the agent tricks. Other labs can take it today and push the number lower, but stay honest about it. The agents also got stuck on the open-ended creative parts, and often just idled instead of iterating. A human researcher still chose the final design. AI sped the search up. It did not replace the scientist. Rewind one year. In early 2025, the same team shipped version 1. 35 volunteers, Spanish sentences they had memorized, and a character error rate of 32% down to 19% for the best subject. It proved the idea could work at all. So what changed in version 2? Version 1 decoded short, rehearsed phrases. Version 2 decodes natural, never seen sentences in real time, a much harder problem, and it still does better. The reason comes down to one word, scale. And this is not just a flashy preprint. The core method was published in Nature Neuroscience, one of the most rigorous journals in the field with independent scientists checking the work. This is vetted research, not a hype reel, and scale is the whole ballgame. Every time you double the brain data, the error drops by a fixed amount, a straight line on a log graph, which means the thing standing between non-invasive reading and surgical grade accuracy may not be biology at all. It might just be more hours of recording. Now remember who this is actually for. Not gamers, not power users, people with ALS, locked-in syndrome, or paralysis, who have lost the ability to speak or move, and for whom brain surgery carries real serious risk every single time. To judge the neural link question, you need to know how the implants work. A surgeon places a grid of electrodes directly onto the surface of the brain, right on the cortex that controls speech or movement. Sitting that close to the neurons, the signal is breathtakingly clean. But that closeness has a price. It needs open brain surgery, with everything that carries. And over months, the body treats the implant as a wound, scar tissue forms, and the once-cleaned signal slowly fades. That degradation is the strongest argument for never going inside at all. Here is where the implants are today. Inside the skull, electrodes have hit speeds over 120 words a minute, with word error rates near 2%. Neural link is targeting 140. That is the gap brain-2 quality is trying to close from the outside. And these two are not the only players. University labs at Stanford and UC Davis have pushed invasive speech implants past 95% accuracy. The whole field is racing. Meta is simply the most serious bet on doing it all without an implant. So two philosophies are fighting here. One says, cut in, place electrodes on the cortex, and get the cleanest possible signal. The other says, never cut at all, read from outside, and close the gap with AI and data instead. Head to head today, it is not closed on every axis. Neural link is faster, it is portable, and it is already living in people right now. Brain-2 quality is slower and tied to a lab. But it needs zero surgery, and it gets better just by adding more data. So does it compete with Neural link? Honestly, not as a product you can buy this year. But as a scientific path, it just proves something huge. You may not have to open the skull to get there at all. The hardware is the next leap. Today's scanner is bulky and cryogenically cooled, which is why it fills a room. But a new kind of sensor, optically pumped magnetometers, works at room temperature inside a flexible helmet. That is the path from a lab rig to something you could one day wear. And why is meta a social media company even doing this? Two reasons, accessibility, genuinely. But also the long game, meta wants neural input for its glasses and headsets. They already ship a wristband that reads the nerves in your arm. The brain is the final frontier of that bet. One honest note, because the headlines will scream mind reading, this cannot be done to you secretly. You have to sit perfectly still in a shielded room, wearing a giant helmet, deliberately trying to type. It reads intended typing, not the quiet voice inside your head, put the trends together, error that scales with data, wearable room temperature sensors, and AI agents building the models. The direction is clear, non-invasive, no surgery brain typing is moving from impossible to merely an engineering problem. So what does it mean for you? Not a gadget on your desk tomorrow. But if you or someone you love ever loses the ability to speak, the odds that they get it back safely without surgery just went meaningfully up.
