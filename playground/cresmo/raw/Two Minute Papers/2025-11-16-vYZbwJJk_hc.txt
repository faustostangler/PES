Now, instead of simulating every thread and every fold like a crazy person, instead, think of this like giving the computer a straw. It’s defined as a Bézier-curve, which enables you to bend and twist this straw easily and smoothly. Now, the algorithm takes a look at this straw, and it makes it thicker or thinner to avoid weird intersections and weird geometries. Then, the poppy thing, the physics simulation shakes it into place, so it gets a natural shape. Man, this is brilliant.

But we are not done yet. Not even close. It then applies continuous collision detection to make sure things don’t intersect into each other. And surprisingly, it doesn’t do it looking frame by frame, like a human would. Nope! It predicts

collisions before they happen, and corrects them instantly. But that is bloody expensive. So, how? Now that is what BVH helps you with. It’s called a bounding volume hierarchy, and when there’s lots going on that might be colliding, it helps us narrow down the search. How? Well,

it does not intersect a high-resolution cloth against another high-resolution cloth, that is a nightmare. It puts these cloths into nice little boxes, I am not even kidding, and finding where these boxes collide is super easy. And fast too. And then, when you find two boxes overlapping, you perform precise collision tests, but only there. Ha! Genius. This is a godsend, saving a ton of computation time.

Now, even this technique has its limitations. It works with templates for these straws, and while new and unusual styles are possible, it takes some modeling in external tools.
