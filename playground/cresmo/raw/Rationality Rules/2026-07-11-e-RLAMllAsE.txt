In 1739, David Hume noticed something that's been quietly haunting moral philosophy ever since. Writers would describe the world, that is the case, this is the case. And then, without warning, slide into what we ought to do. Take a simple example. Asnik will kill you. That is a fact. So don't drink it, feel like it follows. But strictly, it doesn't. Nothing in chemistry tells you what you ought to do about it. No stack of facts, however tall, gets you to a shoot. That's the guillotine. Now, plenty of people think they can jump the gap, drive values straight from the facts, and every attempt either fails or smuggles a value in through the back door. But here's the thing. The guillotine only beheads orts from nowhere. Look back at the Asnik, add one small premise you would want to live, and the orb snaps straight into place. That's how every orb works, anchored to a goal, and once the goal's on the table, the facts do the rest. Hume then didn't kill morality. He told us where it lives, in values. And that's us.
