Alright, today I came here to finally harvest the organic rice I’ve been helping grow! If you haven’t watched the previous videos please watch them too:) Those tiny rice seedlings have grown so big now. I’m honestly so moved! Let's learn about Japanese rice, harvest it, and enjoy a delicious meal together at the end! Harvest Time Today was a special harvest event, so many families from the local area came to join.

Let's goooー! Unlike planting, harvesting doesn’t require any intricate techniques. You simply cut the rice with a sickle—carefully, but boldly. I have to admit, this was my very first time harvesting rice. And as I got into it, I realized something: it felt a lot like cleaning.

Before you start, you think, “This is going to be a lot of work,” but once you begin, you just can’t stop. That feeling of getting into a flow. Apparently, humans release serotonin when they perform simple, repetitive movements, which is why it starts to feel enjoyable. Even though it was around 36°C (about 97°F) and ridiculously hot, before we knew it, everyone was deeply engrossed in cutting the rice. It felt like I had traveled back in time... (laugh)

By the way, Japan has around 1,000 varieties of rice, with about 300 being used specifically for eating. The most famous is “Koshihikari”, followed by “Hitomebore” and “Hinohikari”. The variety we harvested today was called “Niji no Kirameki”, which is quite rare. Tsuji-san, the owner of the farm, is a challenger for new stuff. Lastly, we all took a group photo to commemorate the experience.

Drying Right after being harvested, rice contains about 20-30% moisture. If it has too much moisture, it can’t be stored for long and is prone to spoilage, so it needs to be dried promptly. Here, we dry it using the traditional method of sun drying. By using the natural power of the sun and wind, the rice dries slowly, and the flavors in each grain become even more concentrated.

Enjoy eating! Alright, it's finally time to taste the rice we harvested from this field! Just look at this beautiful, glossy white rice. By the way, I often get asked by foreign friends: “Brown rice is more nutritious, so why do you eat white rice?” Yeah I know ...the simple answer is—it’s tradition.

Of course, most Japanese people are aware that brown rice is more nutritious. And more people here are starting to eat brown rice. But growing up, we’ve been eating white rice. In fact, we love white rice so much that sometimes we even make birthday cakes out of it. Just kidding! Lol

That being said, people didn’t really start eating white rice until the Edo period. Before that, they were eating things like brown rice, barley, and millet.

Alright, Itadakimasu!!! Behold, this is Japan’s famous “tamago kake gohan”(rice with raw egg)! You might think, “Ew, that’s gross!”

But trust me, it’s so delicious, you should try it! If you’re worried about bacteria and don’t want to eat raw egg, you can lightly boil the egg for a soft-boiled version. (Any Japanese Chicken eggs are safe to eat raw) That’s what I used to do when I lived in Canada. Any Japanese chicken eggs are safe to eat raw:) OMG! I kept eating and eating!

When the rice is this good, you can’t stop eating it. In the end, I had four bowls! At Tsuji Farm, they host rice-related events like this, as well as corporate training sessions and rice experience tours. If you’re even a little curious about Japanese rice, why not join in? I’m sure it’ll make you fall in love with real Japanese rice even more!

Lastly: The Power of the Silver Play Button Today, I happened to have my YouTube Silver Play Button with me for filming. When I showed it to the kids, they were beyond excited! The power of YouTube is really something, isn’t it? Seeing them so happy made me happy too! Alright, that’s it for today. See you in the next video!
