  Tthis 1938 home movie might be titled "The Annual Pilgrimage. Natchez, Mississippi. 'Where the Old South Still Lives". Shot in black-and-white and partially in color, it shows various family events before focusing on the Pilgrimage, a yearly event in Natchez, Mississippi, that continues to this day. It began in 1932, when the Natchez Garden Club invited fellow garden clubs from all around the South for a series of garden tours, teas, and luncheons on the grounds of the city's historic houses. Many of the homes' interiors had fallen into disrepair, and tours were supposed to be limited to the exteriors -- until a rainstorm during the Pilgrimage famously changed the thinking of organizers. The event was so popular it became an annual one, and helped ensure the preservation and restoration of many mansions. Today the Natchez Pilgrimage is considered foundational to the city's tourist industry. In the early era the event celebrated and romanticized the "Old South" and antebellum era, with residents dressed in appropriate period costumes. Until the modern era, the portrayal romanticized the plantation era while glossing over the harsh realities of slavery that were foundational to it. The film illustrates how Southern elites in the 1930s used heritage tourism to promote a nostalgic, and often misleading, narrative of the antebellum South. It showcases various historic properties and mansions, highlighting the activities hosted as part of the pilgrimage, which are deeply tied to this complex history.
