  Dating to 1942 and made by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, 
  
  "Farm Battle Lines" demonstrates lesser-known ways in which Southern farmers are contributing to the war effort through harvesting improved strains of cotton for use in explosives, increasing productivity with alternate fertilization methods and utilizing home canning to lessen the amount of food that must be transported to the South. The film was directed by Roger Barlow and narrated by Berry Kroger, with score by Gene Farrell. The film's message is that WWII is not only fought on the battlefield but also on the home front through massive production efforts. It highlights the critical role of farmers (including African Americans / Blacks ), particularly in the South, who contribute to the war by growing cotton for explosives, peanuts for oils and food, and other crops to support both military and civilian needs. Farmers work tirelessly on millions of acres, employing careful soil management and community cooperation to maximize yields. Their labor provides essential raw materials for planes, vehicles, weapons, and strategic construction projects, while also easing transportation burdens through home preservation of food. Ultimately, the film portrays American farmers as indispensable to the war effort, showing that their work in fields, orchards, and pastures directly supports the building of weapons, supplies, and the overall fight for freedom.
