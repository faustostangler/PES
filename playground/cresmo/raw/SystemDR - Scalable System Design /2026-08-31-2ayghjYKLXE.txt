Most engineers who fail the system design interview don't fail on technical knowledge. [music] They fail on scope. This is the discipline that fixes it. The one habit that separates candidates who go deep from candidates who just run out the clock. Here's the failure mode.

A candidate is asked to design Twitter, the whole thing. Timeline, search, notifications, recommendations, ads, analytics, direct messages. 40 minutes in, they're still drawing boxes on the whiteboard. They haven't gone deep on a single piece of it. And the scorecard doesn't say weak distributed systems.

>> [music] >> It says breadth over depth, no clear prioritization. That's not a knowledge gap. It's a scoping problem, and it's the single most common failure mode among technically strong candidates. Most candidates spend the first 5 minutes asking clarifying questions. How many [music] users? What's the read

write ratio? Does this need global availability? Those are [music] fine questions, but they're generic. They could be asked of any system on earth, so they don't actually signal that you know how to manage scope. >> [music] >> What separates the candidates who score well is that they don't just ask questions. They propose scope. Something

like, "There are several interesting subsystems here. I'm going to focus on the timeline and the fanout architecture. I'll acknowledge the rest exist, but I won't design them in detail. Does that work [music] for you?" That's a completely different move.

You're telling the interviewer what you will and won't design, and inviting them to redirect if they disagree. Most will say yes. Some will redirect you towards something more specific. And that's information you'd never have gotten by only asking questions. Why does the instinct to cover everything feel so strong in the first place? Because it

seems like the interview is testing your breadth. >> [music] >> And if you only design one component, won't the interviewer assume you don't know the rest? That misreads what's actually being scored. At L5 and above, interviewers already assume you're familiar with notifications and search. You work as a software engineer. That's

a given. What they can't tell from your resume is whether you can identify the hardest distributed systems problem hiding in the question and go deep enough on it to prove real understanding. >> [music] >> Designing eight systems shallowly proves you're familiar with eight systems. Designing one system deeply proves you can think. The second one is what gets scored. [music] So, how do you pick the

one thing to go deep on? Not the component you know best. Not the one that feels safest to talk about. The component where the answer is genuinely non-obvious, where the interesting distributed systems problem actually lives. For a system like Twitter, the fanout problem beats the user profile service every time. [music]

The timeline cache architecture beats the follow-unfollow API. A useful test to run in your head. If I describe this component at a surface level, would the interviewer immediately want to probe deeper? If yes, that's your starting point. And the components you're not designing, you don't ignore them. You

acknowledge [music] them. Take notifications. You could say, "This would follow a standard fanout on write pattern. Push to APNs and FCM for mobile, web socket for web, similar to what we just covered for the timeline. [music] I won't design it in detail, but the interesting problem there is the delivery guarantee under failure. You'd

need at least once delivery with client-side dedupe." One sentence. It shows you understand the problem, the standard solution, and the sharp edge case without spending 5 minutes building a design you don't need. Here's the exact framing that ties it together. After you've done estimation, before you draw a single box, say this out loud.

"Before I draw the architecture, I want to lock scope. I'm going to go deep on this component because that's where the interesting distributed systems problem is. I'll acknowledge these other components exist and show where they connect, but I won't design them fully unless you'd like me to. Does that [music] work?" This scope lock does two things. It sets expectations. So, the

interviewer isn't sitting there waiting for you to cover something you've already decided to skip. And it gives them one last chance to redirect you before you've sunk 30 minutes into the wrong area. Once that lock lands, it's [music] locked. Don't drift from it. And here's the part candidates get wrong even after they've nailed the scope lock. If you finish your deep dive with

time to spare, the instinct is to fill the silence by adding a new component. Don't. That just reopens the exact breadth trap you proposed your way out of 5 minutes earlier. The better move, every time, is to go deeper on the component you already chose. More rigor on the hard problem is always worth more than surface coverage of a new one, which gets at the uncomfortable truth about breadth. It is valuable to

demonstrate, but demonstrating it through shallow coverage of many components is the wrong way to earn that credit. The right way is through the quality of your acknowledgements. Spending 5 minutes on a shallow design of some side component gets you a certain amount of credit. Naming that same component's core problem and its standard solution in one sharp sentence gets you the same signal, 5 minutes [music] cheaper. Scope discipline was never about knowing less. It's about

deploying what you already know efficiently enough that depth shows up exactly where it's being scored.
