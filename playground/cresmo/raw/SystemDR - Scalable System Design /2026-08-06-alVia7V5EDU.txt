Reach for AutoML when you're working with tabular data and a well-defined metric with a clean baseline. When your team needs a strong baseline fast before investing further engineering time. When compute budget is available, but engineering time is the scarcer resource. When you need to retrain many similar models on a cadence. Think per market or per tenant models.

Or when the search space is large, but well-studied. Like standard gradient boosting or common neural network blocks. Stay manual when domain structure, causal constraints, monotonicity requirements >> [music] >> needs to directly shape the model. When your data is small, imbalanced, or prone to leakage because search will happily overfit to the leaderboard in those conditions. When interpretability or a regulatory audit requires a pipeline your team fully understands. Not just a winning

configuration. When the problem is genuinely novel, so there's no strong prior for a search algorithm to lean on. Or when your latency or memory budget is tight enough that it requires hand-tuned tradeoffs a generic search won't discover on its own. >> [music] >> In practice, many teams run both in parallel early on. AutoML for a fast baseline, [music] and manual tuning to see whether it's actually worth trying to beat that baseline.

Now, [music] let's talk about the failure modes that don't show up in a vendor demo. First, compute runaway. An unbounded search on a large tabular data set can burn a full GPU day chasing a fraction of a percent of AUC improvement.
