So, let's [music] fix one shared mental model before we go any further, because everything else in this talk builds [music] on it. Information retrieval answers one question: Which documents are relevant? You give it a corpus [music] and a query, and it hands you back a ranked list of documents. It's reasoning at the level of the whole document. [music] It never actually has to understand what's inside a page, only estimate how likely that page is to satisfy the query. [music]

That's why its evaluation metrics are things like precision at K, recall, and NDCG. [music] All about ranking quality, not correctness of any single fact. Information extraction answers a completely different [music] question: What facts does this text actually state? You give it unstructured text and it hands you back structured tuples, a graph of entities, [music] spans, and relations. It's reasoning at a much finer grain, individual [music] entities and the relationships between them, which is exactly why it's more computationally and label intensive than IR. [music]

Its metrics reflect that, too: slot F1, entity F1, exact match. All about whether the [music] structure it committed to is actually correct. Neither one is harder in some abstract [music] sense. They're solving genuinely different problems. If you ever catch yourself comparing accuracy numbers [music] across an IE system and an IR system, that's usually a sign the comparison itself doesn't make sense.
