El Nino is upon us. What is it? And why do we need to keep an eye on it? First, we need to understand trade winds. They span the entire planet and blow from east to west. In the Pacific Ocean they help transport warm surface water to Asia and Oceania, allowing cold as sub-surface water to flow towards South America. But every two to seven years, the Pacific plays a UNO reverse card. As a result of a complex interplay between atmospheric pressure and ocean surface temperature, the trade winds we can all even change direction and the warm water sloshes back to the east and Pacific. If the average temperature of the local sea surface changes by more than 0.5 degrees Celsius, it's called an El Nino event. In South America, El Nino leads to warmer ocean water and heavy rains, while Asia and Oceania experience an increased risk of droughts. Due to climate change, ocean surface temperatures have already risen 0.9 degrees Celsius since pre-industrial times. With El Nino adding even more surface heat, the consequences for marine life are even worse, eventually reducing the ocean's ability to absorb CO2, bad news for an already warming planet.
