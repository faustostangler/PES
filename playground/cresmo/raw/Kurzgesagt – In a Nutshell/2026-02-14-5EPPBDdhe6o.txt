This worm has one more Nobel prizes than anyone else. Meet Sea Elegans. A worm so small, it could pass through the eye of a needle. Thanks to its transparent body and speedy life cycle, scientists were able to track every one of its 959 cells from cradle to grave, building the first complete blueprint of a living organism. They even mapped to all of its neurons and their connections, creating the first connectum, kickstarting the science of brain mapping and unlocking powerful genetic tools. Sea Elegans was also the first animal where green fluorescent protein was used to track gene activity. When certain genes were switched on, the worms would literally light up. It even helped us in switching off specific genes, which became a key tool in genetics and drug development. By helping us define the genes that tell cells when to grow, divide or die, it brought us closer to understanding diseases like cancer. This worm even went to space, helping scientists explore how microgravity affects muscles, aging and DNA repair. If a tiny worm can help win four Nobel prizes, surely the rest of us can aim for at least one.
