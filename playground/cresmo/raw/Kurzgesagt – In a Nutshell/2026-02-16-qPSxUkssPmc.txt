Polio is back, but why? A global vaccination campaign has cornered wild polio to just two countries, but this paralysis-causing disease is not going down without a fight. Though we have vaccines to tackle it, conflict and healthcare access make full coverage and uphill struggle, and rare wild polio detections outside these areas show progress is still fragile. And we have an even bigger problem. Wherever there are immunity gaps, we can forms the virus from the oral polio vaccine, but can spread and in very rare cases evolve back into forms that cause paratosis. These outnumber wild cases, and polio's long incubation period lets it spread undetected between the unvaccinated for weeks. The good news is we now have vaccines whose virus is a lot less likely to mutate, and over two billion doses have been given out. But to truly end polio, we need to reach almost everyone. That means every kid who hasn't had a first dose even in war zones. One single case is a sign that an outbreak may have begun, so we need to keep up surveillance and track the virus in real time. We've come so far with around 20 million children walking today who would have been paralyzed and 1.6 million lives saved. But if we lose momentum, a future with hundreds of thousands of new cases is just around the corner.
