Right now, the universe is lush and vibrant, alive with color and light from galaxies and stars, just like it has been for over 10 billion years. It won't always be like this, though. Our universe is dying, growing older and larger and colder with each passing year. Though it's happening too slowly for us to notice, over eons, our universe will become unrecognizable as it dies. Let's fast forward through the future to see exactly how it will unfold. When the universe is 10 times

older, the pretty spiral galaxies we're used to seeing today will be virtually extinct. Gravity has long since pulled galaxy groups together, their stars mixing away into giant blobby elliptical galaxies. The spiral galaxies are today the star factories of our universe, pulling together gas in their arms to make new stars. In a future without them, star formation slows, though doesn't stop. As stars die, they return much of their gas to their surroundings, providing a pinch of fuel to extend the life of the galaxy. And it's not just

our Milky Way. Every galaxy does this, and they're now getting quite far apart. Galaxies right next door today will be driven billions of light years away by the accelerating expansion of the universe. Galaxies become islands in an increasingly dark and cold universe. Let's jump forward even further. When

the universe is tens of trillions of years old, other galaxies have become faint memories. The expansion of the universe has driven them all beyond the cosmic event horizon where they are at such great distances that the space between galaxies grows faster even than the speed of light. No light emitted from one galaxy can ever again make it to another. Every galaxy is alone in an endless dark void. Within what remains of our galaxy, the last star has long since formed and the once raging galactic fire has burned down to embers.

Only the smallest, longest lived stars still shine. And with each passing moment, they grow older. Let's jump forward again. The last stars are now dead, and the galaxy is a dark ball of invisibly cold white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes silently orbiting about the galactic center. But not forever. Though the odds are small, at

any given time, stars have a chance to collide, providing a rare blip of light and excitement. Over time, the galaxy runs out of objects as those black holes gobble up the remaining matter until they too eventually are gobbled up by the super massive black hole in the center. And then, with our observable universe containing only one single ultramassive black hole, we wait. By radiating small numbers of particles near their event horizon in a process called Hawking radiation, black holes can lose mass, albeit very slowly. The last final black hole in the universe may live an unimaginably long time, but not forever until finally evaporating away entirely in one final burst of light. And with that, our universe is

dead. Cold and empty forever.
