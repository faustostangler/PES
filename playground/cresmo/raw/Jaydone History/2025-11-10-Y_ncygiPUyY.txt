Roscoe Fadi Arbuckle was one of the most famous silent film comedians of the early 1900s. His career came crashing down in 1921 when he was accused of sexually abusing and killing actress Virginia Rap during a party at the St. Francis Hotel in San Francisco. The case shocked the nation and became one of Hollywood's earliest and most sensational scandals. Arbuckle was charged with manslaughter after Rap died from internal injuries, days after the party. The prosecution claimed he had assaulted her during the gathering, while the defense argued that Rap's death was due to a pre-existing medical condition. Witness testimony conflicted heavily, in the media painted Arbuckle as both a villain and a victim depending on the outlets. The first trial in 1921 ended with a hung jury, as jurors could not agree on whether Arbuckle was guilty. A second trial also ended without a verdict. By the third trial, Arbuckle's defense team had successfully exposed contradictions in witness statements and proved that the evidence did not conclusively tie him to Rap's death. The jury deliberated only six minutes before declaring him not guilty. Though the court cleared him, the damage was done. The public outrage forced film studios to cut ties with him, and his films were banned for years. Arbuckle's trials changed Hollywood forever, leading to the creation of morality clauses in actor contracts and pushing the film industry toward stricter censorship policies.
