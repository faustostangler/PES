This is the most insanely satisfying problem I have ever seen. A square is divided into several regions as shown, with the middle square region shaded. Few of the lengths are equal in size as well. The question is, what fraction of the entire square is this middle shaded square region? You know what? At first, this looks like a problem where we might need to calculate several angles, side lengths, or areas. But there is actually a much simpler trick hiding in the diagram. I would like you all to pause this video and try to solve it on your own. Actually, the key is to look at the four right triangles along the edges of the square. Then take each of these four right triangles and rotate it by 180 degrees. Then attach each rotated triangle to the trapezoid next to it like this. Oh, wow! The beautiful part is that each triangle fits perfectly into its neighboring region. After doing this for all four triangles, the original square can be rearranged into five congruent squares. But wait, how do we know those four new regions really have the same area as the shaded square? They could potentially be rectangles that are slightly larger or smaller, right? No, they cannot, because the lines are connected at the midpoints of the large square. This fixes the lengths of the triangles where the hypotenuse is half the side of the large square while one leg is exactly the side of one of the smaller squares, which fixes the other leg as well. The shaded region is therefore one of those five equal squares. Hence, the required fraction is one fifth or twenty percent of the entire square and that's the whole trick.
