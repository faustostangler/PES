May 18, 1980, 9:00am. Lee Jung-ho, an engineering student at Chonnam University in the city of Gwangju, South Korea, is doing some homework when his roommate Chung Sung-Min bursts into their dorm. Sung-min cries. (A, Ch. 2) Jung-ho shrugs. In October, the country’s longtime dictator Park Chung-hee was

assassinated by his own spy chief. In December Park’s successor, Chun doo-Hwan, launched a military coup to secure power. And now, Chun is enacting martial law. (A, Ch. 1) This inflames Sung-min, who hoped that Park's death would finally bring democracy to Korea.

Jung-ho feels differently. Sung-min is disgusted.

Sung-min departs. Jung-ho spends the rest of the day working in his room. At one point, he sees troops marching by his window. As the hours pass, he hears sirens. (A, Ch. 2)

Jung-ho hopes Sung-min is alright. He can be annoying, but he’s a good guy. But Sung-min doesn’t return that evening—strange, for a Sunday. After asking around, Jung-ho learns that protestors and troops clashed violently in the city, and that Gwangju is now under a curfew for the night. (A, Ch. 2)

Jung-ho is worried, but now he can’t leave his dormitory. Sung-min is probably fine, he reassures himself. Things will blow over by tomorrow. But Jung-ho is wrong. Events in the city of Gwangju are not calming down. After years of

brutal repression, the citizens of Gwangju are about to fight for their freedom, in one of the most inspiring and tragic episodes in the entire history of democracy. And Sung-min is right. Jung-ho can’t stay out of it forever. Whether he wants to or not, like all people in difficult and dangerous times, he’ll need to pick a side. This is the Gwangju uprising.

The next morning, May 19, Jung-ho woke up in his room to find that Sung-min still hadn’t returned. Worried, he ventured into the city to find his roommate. The streets outside Chonnam were unusually empty, but he heard chanting in the distance. At first, he couldn’t make sense of it, but as he got closer to the center of Gwangju, it became clear. “Down with Chun doo-Hwan! Down with Chun doo-Hwan!” (A, Ch. 2)

Protestors—not just students, but shopkeepers, retail employees, and homemakers—filled the street, brandishing banners and chanting slogans. Facing them were paratroopers of the Korean Army, armed with batons and riot shields. (A, Ch. 3) Packed in a tight formation, one group of soldiers rammed into the protestors, slamming down their batons. A few protestors picked up rocks and hurled them at the troops, but it wasn’t enough to stop the soldiers from dragging bloody protestors off to the side, where they were further kicked, beaten, then cuffed and herded into military trucks. (A, Ch. 3)

The next day, May 20th, Jung-ho and his uncle followed his aunt’s advice, staying inside. Even far from the city center, they heard shouting and more sirens. They turned on the radio, listening for an update, but neither the local nor the national radio station had anything to say about what was happening. (A, Ch. 4) A neighbor came by in the early evening, asking if Jung-ho’s uncle was planning to join all of the taxi drivers at Mudeung Sports Center.

Though they arrived within minutes, Jung-ho’s hope for his friend was dwindling. He watched as nurses put Sung-min on a gurney and wheeled him away. He and his uncle sat quietly in the waiting room, densely packed with the family and friends of the mounting casualties of the clashes.

At one point, his uncle left to bring word of what happened to his aunt and to let her know that they were both alive. Jung-ho stayed in the waiting room until a doctor came out. Quietly and compassionately, she let him know that his roommate had not made it. Just as she delivered this terrible news, a fellow student, one Jung-ho recognized, burst into the room. (A, Ch. 5)

At 2am May 27th, the troops began their final advance into the city. Their tanks, superior training, and numbers made their operation a foregone conclusion. By 4am Province Hall was surrounded. Troops made their way in, sweeping the building, commanding surrender, and shooting anyone who fled or made any sign of resistance. (A, Ch. 13)

By 7:30, the operation concluded. Yoon Sang-won, like hundreds of others, did not survive the Gwangju uprising. He was shot and killed by Korean troops. (A, Ch. 13) Lee Jung-ho, though he suffered terrible beatings and a time in prison, survived.

When he got out in 1983, Chun Doo-hwan’s dictatorship was totally secured. Jung-ho knew the time for heroics had passed, so he finished his education and landed a job at a large Korean engineering firm. He worked in Seoul, and he kept his head down, visiting Gwangju occasionally to see his aunt and uncle and to visit the grave of his friend, Chung Sung-min. Then, in 1987, something unexpected happened. One Saturday evening in June, Lee Jung-ho turned on the radio in his suburban apartment and learned that protests had erupted throughout downtown Seoul. Hundreds of thousands were in the street. He grabbed his coat,

ran out of his building, and hailed a taxi. Within days, thousands had been arrested, but before long Chun Doo-hwan realized he couldn't keep it up. On June 29, the government conceded to the massive protests and promised the return of free and fair elections to Korea. (A, Ch. 14-15) The dream of Gwangju had finally come to pass. Lee Jung-ho stood amidst the

crowds and shed a tear as he remembered his old friend. Chung Sung-min would be proud.
