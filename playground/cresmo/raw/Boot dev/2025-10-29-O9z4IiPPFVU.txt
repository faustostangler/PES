If you want to go to a friend's house, the simplest way is typically for them to send you their home address. You pop it into Google Maps and off you go. The internet, or computer networks in general, are similar to a city full of houses. Each house has an absolute location, a latitude longitude coordinate, and a label that makes it easier to find that location. No one wants to memorize 37.06

or -113.40. So, instead we use a human-friendly address, like 356 Huckleberry Drive, and say that address maps to that latitude longitude coordinate. Computers work the same way. An IP address is the computer-friendly address of a machine on a network, like the internet. For example, 198.35.26.96.

When you visit a website, you're really just making a request to a computer, called a server, that's configured to serve web pages. But it's inconvenient to tell your friend, "Hey, I found this cool site. Go to 132.148.74.10."

That's where domain names and DNS, the domain name system, come into play. DNS is like Google Maps in our house example. It takes a human-friendly address, like wikipedia.org, and tells your computer the actual IP address it needs to talk to to get to that human-friendly address. And that human-friendly address, wikipedia.org,

it's called a domain name. Domain names are for humans, so we don't have to memorize the IP addresses of all the servers we want to contact. It's also worth mentioning that 132.148.74.10

is an IPv4 address, Internet Protocol version 4. But IPv6, or Internet Protocol version 6, is also very commonly used now. And those addresses are practically impossible to remember. I mean, look at this thing. [Music]
