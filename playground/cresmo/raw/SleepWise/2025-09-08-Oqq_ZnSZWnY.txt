In reality, a camel's hump is a fat reserve, not a reservoir of water. Each hump stores up to 35 kg of fat, which can be metabolized into both energy and small amounts of water when food is scarce. This adaptation allows camels to endure long journeys across barren landscapes without needing constant access to vegetation. When the fat reserve is depleted, the hump visibly shrinks or droops, showing its true role as stored nutrition. Camels manage water in other ways. They

can drink up to 40 gallons in a single sitting and rehydrate quickly without suffering the shock that would harm most mammals. Their blood cells are ovalshaped rather than round, allowing them to circulate efficiently even when dehydrated. They also minimize water loss by producing concentrated urine and dry feces and by tolerating body temperature fluctuations that reduce sweating in extreme heat. Scientific studies of camel physiology emphasize these remarkable adaptations which together explain their desert resilience far better than the myth of water storing humps. The myth persists because the hump is so visually distinctive and invites a simple explanation.

The fact to remember is that camels store fat, not water, in their humps. In practice, this means their survival depends on efficient water use, not hidden reservoirs.
