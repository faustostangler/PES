Planned obsolescence. The frustration

you feel when your phone slows down exactly 2 years after you bought it is a manufactured emotion. It is the result of a business strategy designed to turn your durable goods into disposable trash. This concept began in a secret meeting in Geneva in 1924. The world's biggest light bulb manufacturers realized their products were becoming too good. bulbs were lasting practically forever, which meant sales were stagnating.

To fix this problem, they formed a cartel and agreed to lower the lifespan of a light bulb to exactly 1,000 hours. They didn't engineer a better bulb. They engineered a fragile one. This was the birth of planned obsolescence. Today, this philosophy is baked into the design of almost everything you own. It

has evolved from fragile filaments to unrepaiable barriers. Engineers are often instructed to use heavy amounts of glue instead of screws, ensuring that opening a device to fix a battery destroys the screen. They use proprietary screws that no standard screwdriver can turn. Software updates are rolled out that demand more processing power than older hardware can handle, making a perfectly functional device feel sluggish and obsolete. The goal is to shift your status from an owner to a consumer. An owner maintains

and repairs. A consumer uses and discards. Companies rely on this cycle of waste to maintain infinite growth. If they sold you a washing machine that lasted 40 years as they did in the midentth century, they would lose decades of future sales. assume that new technology must inherently replace the old. But the reality is much simpler. Your devices aren't dying because we lack the technology to keep them alive. They are dying because your need for a replacement is the product they are actually selling.
